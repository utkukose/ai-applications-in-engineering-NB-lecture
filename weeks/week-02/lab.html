Path planning playground: Search on grids and terrain

Prof. Dr. Utku Kose, Süleyman Demirel University

Course homeWeek overviewLecture pageColab notebookPDF notes

Part A is a grid on which walls and slow terrain are painted with the pointer. Breadth-first search, uniform-cost search, greedy best-first search, A* and weighted A* run on the same map, animated step by step, and report path cost and expanded cells [1, 2]. Part B plans a route across a synthetic valley in which each move costs distance plus a slope penalty, and moves steeper than a grade limit are forbidden, as in haul-road and pipeline design. Part C runs breadth-first search, uniform-cost search, A*, weighted A* and greedy best-first search step by step on the road network of the lecture and checks whether the heuristic is admissible. Part D shows every decision of simulated annealing on a cost profile, and Part E counts the nodes of search trees and the orders of jobs [2, 3].

Part A: Algorithms on a grid

Green is the start, red the goal. Light blue cells were expanded; the orange line is the returned path. Breadth-first search ignores the cost of slow terrain.

Part B: A route across a valley

Darker cells are higher. The route starts on the left and ends on the right. Raise the slope penalty to see the route follow contour lines; lower the grade limit to see which slopes become impassable.

Part C: Search step by step on the road network

The network of the worked examples is drawn on the left. Each click expands one node and lists every cost update with the formulas of the lecture, g(n') = g(n) + c(n, n') and f(n) = g(n) + h(n); the frontier is sorted by the priority of the chosen algorithm. The road costs can be edited: Lowering a cost below the straight-line distance makes the heuristic inadmissible, and the table at the bottom shows where [1, 2].

Part D: Simulated annealing decision by decision

A single solution x moves along a cost profile with several valleys. Each step proposes a neighbour, computes the change in cost and, for a worsening, the acceptance probability e−Δ/T, and compares it with a random number. A start temperature of zero turns the method into hill climbing, which stops in the nearest valley [3].

Part E: How fast do search trees and orders grow?

The table adds up the levels of a complete search tree, checks the sum against the closed formula of the lecture and counts the extra nodes of iterative deepening. The second table builds the factorial n! by one multiplication per job [4, 5].
