Time series in engineering

A time series is a sequence of measurements indexed by time. Its behaviour usually combines a trend, a slow change of level; seasonality, a pattern that repeats with a fixed period such as a day or a year; cycles without a fixed period; and irregular noise. Neighbouring values are correlated, which is the property forecasting exploits and the property that breaks the independence assumption of the random splits used in Weeks 7 and 8. Box and Jenkins built a systematic methodology on autocorrelation and differencing [1], and Hyndman and Athanasopoulos give a modern, practical account of forecasting with open examples [2].

The Keeling curve is a textbook series. Continuous measurements at Mauna Loa since 1958 show the carbon dioxide concentration rising every year, with an annual cycle of a few parts per million caused by the growth and decay of vegetation in the northern hemisphere [3, 4]. The rise itself accelerates, so a straight line underestimates the future.

Formulas: Components, differences and autocorrelation

A time series y1,y2,…,yTy_1, y_2, \ldots, y_Ty1​,y2​,…,yT​ is often described as the sum of a trend TtT_tTt​, a seasonal component StS_tSt​ that repeats with the period mmm, for example m=12m = 12m=12 for monthly data with a yearly cycle, and a remainder RtR_tRt​ [2]:

yt=Tt+St+Rty_t = T_t + S_t + R_tyt​=Tt​+St​+Rt​

When the seasonal swings grow with the level, a product yt=Tt×St×Rty_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_tyt​=Tt​×St​×Rt​ fits better, and taking logarithms turns it into a sum. Differences remove a trend or a season:

∇yt=yt−yt−1,    ∇myt=yt−yt−m\nabla y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}, \;\; \nabla_m y_t = y_t - y_{t-m}∇yt​=yt​−yt−1​,∇m​yt​=yt​−yt−m​

The autocorrelation at lag kkk measures how strongly the series is correlated with itself kkk steps earlier. With the mean y‾\overline{y}y​ of the series [1]:

rk=∑t=k+1T(yt−y‾)(yt−k−y‾)∑t=1T(yt−y‾)2r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T} (y_t - \overline{y})(y_{t-k} - \overline{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{T} (y_t - \overline{y})^2}rk​=∑t=1T​(yt​−y​)2∑t=k+1T​(yt​−y​)(yt−k​−y​)​

For a series without any autocorrelation, about 95% of the values rkr_krk​ lie between −1.96/T-1.96/\sqrt{T}−1.96/T​ and 1.96/T1.96/\sqrt{T}1.96/T​ [2]. A seasonal series has large values of rkr_krk​ at multiples of the period, and a trending series has values that decrease slowly with kkk.

Baselines and honest evaluation

Every forecasting study needs baselines. The naive forecast repeats the last observation, and the seasonal naive forecast repeats the value from one season earlier, for example the same month of the previous year [2]. A sophisticated model that cannot beat them has learned nothing useful. Evaluation must respect time: The model is trained on data up to a forecast origin and tested on the following horizon. Rolling-origin evaluation repeats this for several origins and averages the errors, which gives a more reliable picture than a single split. Errors grow with the horizon, so results should be reported per horizon or for the horizon that matters for the decision, such as one day ahead for unit commitment in a power system [5].

Formulas: Baselines and error measures

A forecast made at the origin TTT for the horizon hhh is written y^T+h∣T\hat{y}_{T+h \vert T}y^​T+h∣T​. The naive forecast repeats the last observation, the seasonal naive forecast repeats the observation of the same season one period earlier, here for horizons up to one period, and the drift method extends the average change of the past [2]:

y^T+h∣T=yT,    y^T+h∣T=yT+h−m,    y^T+h∣T=yT+h yT−y1T−1\hat{y}_{T+h \vert T} = y_T, \;\; \hat{y}_{T+h \vert T} = y_{T+h-m}, \;\; \hat{y}_{T+h \vert T} = y_T + h\, \frac{y_T - y_1}{T - 1}y^​T+h∣T​=yT​,y^​T+h∣T​=yT+h−m​,y^​T+h∣T​=yT​+hT−1yT​−y1​​

With the errors et=yt−y^te_t = y_t - \hat{y}_tet​=yt​−y^​t​ of the nnn test values, the common error measures are

MAE=1n∑∣et∣,    RMSE=1n∑et2,    MAPE=100n∑∣etyt∣\text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n} \sum \vert e_t \vert, \;\; \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum e_t^2}, \;\; \text{MAPE} = \frac{100}{n} \sum \Big\vert \frac{e_t}{y_t} \Big\vertMAE=n1​∑∣et​∣,RMSE=n1​∑et2​​,MAPE=n100​∑​yt​et​​​

MAE is in the unit of the data, RMSE weighs large errors more, and MAPE is a percentage that fails when values come close to zero. The mean absolute scaled error divides the MAE by the in-sample MAE of the seasonal naive forecast on the training data, so that values below 1 beat that baseline [2]:

MASE=MAE1T−m∑t=m+1T∣yt−yt−m∣\text{MASE} = \frac{\text{MAE}}{\frac{1}{T - m} \sum_{t=m+1}^{T} \vert y_t - y_{t-m} \vert}MASE=T−m1​∑t=m+1T​∣yt​−yt−m​∣MAE​

Rolling-origin evaluation repeats the forecast for several origins T1<T2<⋯T_1 < T_2 < \cdotsT1​<T2​<⋯ and averages the errors for each horizon.

Regression models for time series

Linear regression becomes a forecasting model when its inputs describe time. A polynomial trend captures slow change, and pairs of sine and cosine terms with the seasonal period, called Fourier terms, capture a smooth seasonal pattern with few parameters. Lagged values of the series itself, the value one month ago or twelve months ago, turn forecasting into ordinary supervised learning, so that ridge regression, random forests or gradient boosting can be used. For horizons longer than one step, the model is applied recursively, feeding its own forecasts back as lags, or a separate model is trained for each horizon. Exponential smoothing and ARIMA models are classical alternatives with well understood behaviour and prediction intervals [1, 2].

Formulas: Trend and Fourier terms, autoregression and exponential smoothing

A regression on time describes the trend by powers of ttt and a season of period mmm by pairs of sine and cosine terms, the Fourier terms [2]:

yt=β0+β1t+β2t2+∑k=1K[aksin⁡2πktm+bkcos⁡2πktm]+εty_t = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 t^2 + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \Big[ a_k \sin\frac{2 \pi k t}{m} + b_k \cos\frac{2 \pi k t}{m} \Big] + \varepsilon_tyt​=β0​+β1​t+β2​t2+k=1∑K​[ak​sinm2πkt​+bk​cosm2πkt​]+εt​

The pair of order k=1k = 1k=1 describes one smooth wave per period. Since asin⁡θ+bcos⁡θ=Asin⁡(θ+ϕ)a \sin \theta + b \cos \theta = A \sin(\theta + \phi)asinθ+bcosθ=Asin(θ+ϕ) with A=a2+b2A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}A=a2+b2​, the pair has the amplitude AAA, and its phase sets the season of the peak; higher orders sharpen the shape.

An autoregression predicts a value from the ppp values before it, which turns forecasting into ordinary regression on lagged inputs [1]:

yt=c+ϕ1yt−1+ϕ2yt−2+⋯+ϕpyt−p+εty_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \cdots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_tyt​=c+ϕ1​yt−1​+ϕ2​yt−2​+⋯+ϕp​yt−p​+εt​

For p=1p = 1p=1, least squares on the pairs (xt,yt)=(yt−1,yt)(x_t, y_t) = (y_{t-1}, y_t)(xt​,yt​)=(yt−1​,yt​) gives ϕ=Sxy/Sxx\phi = S_{xy}/S_{xx}ϕ=Sxy​/Sxx​ and c=y‾−ϕ x‾c = \overline{y} - \phi\, \overline{x}c=y​−ϕx, with

Sxy=∑xtyt−1n∑xt∑yt,    Sxx=∑xt2−1n(∑xt)2S_{xy} = \sum x_t y_t - \frac{1}{n} \sum x_t \sum y_t, \;\; S_{xx} = \sum x_t^2 - \frac{1}{n} \Big(\sum x_t\Big)^2Sxy​=∑xt​yt​−n1​∑xt​∑yt​,Sxx​=∑xt2​−n1​(∑xt​)2

for the nnn pairs. Forecasts for several steps use earlier forecasts as inputs, recursively. For ∣ϕ∣<1\vert \phi \vert < 1∣ϕ∣<1 they approach the mean μ=c/(1−ϕ)\mu = c/(1 - \phi)μ=c/(1−ϕ) geometrically, since y^T+h−μ=ϕh(yT−μ)\hat{y}_{T+h} - \mu = \phi^h (y_T - \mu)y^​T+h​−μ=ϕh(yT​−μ).

Exponential smoothing forms a level ℓt\ell_tℓt​ as a weighted average of the newest value and the previous level, with a smoothing factor 0<α≤10 < \alpha \le 10<α≤1; unrolled, the weights of the past values α,α(1−α),α(1−α)2,…\alpha, \alpha(1 - \alpha), \alpha(1 - \alpha)^2, \ldotsα,α(1−α),α(1−α)2,… decay geometrically. Holt's method adds a trend btb_tbt​ with its own factor β\betaβ [2]:

ℓt=αyt+(1−α)(ℓt−1+bt−1),    bt=β(ℓt−ℓt−1)+(1−β)bt−1\ell_t = \alpha y_t + (1 - \alpha)(\ell_{t-1} + b_{t-1}), \;\; b_t = \beta (\ell_t - \ell_{t-1}) + (1 - \beta) b_{t-1}ℓt​=αyt​+(1−α)(ℓt−1​+bt−1​),bt​=β(ℓt​−ℓt−1​)+(1−β)bt−1​
y^T+h∣T=ℓT+h bT\hat{y}_{T+h \vert T} = \ell_T + h\, b_Ty^​T+h∣T​=ℓT​+hbT​

Without the trend, bt=0b_t = 0bt​=0, this is simple exponential smoothing, whose forecast is the last level for every horizon. The Holt-Winters method adds a third equation for the seasonal component.

Recurrent neural networks

A recurrent network processes a sequence one step at a time and carries a hidden state from step to step, so that it can summarise the past in a fixed-size vector [6]. Training by backpropagation through time suffers from vanishing and exploding gradients, which makes long-range dependencies hard to learn [7]. The long short-term memory cell adds a memory cell whose input and output gates control what is written into it and read from it, and a forget gate, added in later work, controls what is kept; this design lets information and gradients flow across many steps [8]. The gated recurrent unit is a simpler relative [9]. Convolutional networks over time and attention-based transformers are strong alternatives for sequence modelling [10, 11], and surveys compare these families for forecasting [12].

Deep sequence models need data. A monthly series of about eight hundred values is small for an LSTM, and a well-designed regression with seasonal terms is often as accurate. Recurrent networks show their strength with many related series, such as the load of thousands of feeders, and with long high-frequency records, such as the degradation trajectories of turbofan engines in the C-MAPSS simulation data used for remaining useful life prediction [13].

Formulas: The recurrent unit, its gradient and the LSTM cell

A recurrent network reads the inputs x1,x2,…x_1, x_2, \ldotsx1​,x2​,… one at a time and updates a hidden state hth_tht​ from the new input and the previous state, with the same weights at every step [6]:

ht=tanh⁡(Wxxt+Whht−1+b),    y^t=Wyht+ch_t = \tanh(W_x x_t + W_h h_{t-1} + b), \;\; \hat{y}_t = W_y h_t + cht​=tanh(Wx​xt​+Wh​ht−1​+b),y^​t​=Wy​ht​+c

Backpropagation through time applies the chain rule along the sequence. For a single unit with the weight whw_hwh​, the derivative of a later state with respect to an earlier one is a product of one factor per step:

∂ht∂ht−k=∏j=t−k+1twh (1−hj2)\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} = \prod_{j=t-k+1}^{t} w_h\, (1 - h_j^2)∂ht−k​∂ht​​=j=t−k+1∏t​wh​(1−hj2​)

If the factors are smaller than 1 in size, the product vanishes exponentially with kkk; if they are larger, it explodes. Either way, dependencies over many steps are hard to learn [7].

Hochreiter and Schmidhuber introduced a memory cell ctc_tct​ whose content is changed only by addition, protected by gates [8]. The form used today, for example in PyTorch, has an input gate iti_tit​, a forget gate ftf_tft​, an output gate oto_tot​ and a candidate gtg_tgt​, each computed from xtx_txt​ and ht−1h_{t-1}ht−1​ with its own weights:

it=σ(Wixt+Uiht−1+bi),    ft=σ(Wfxt+Ufht−1+bf)i_t = \sigma(W_i x_t + U_i h_{t-1} + b_i), \;\; f_t = \sigma(W_f x_t + U_f h_{t-1} + b_f)it​=σ(Wi​xt​+Ui​ht−1​+bi​),ft​=σ(Wf​xt​+Uf​ht−1​+bf​)
gt=tanh⁡(Wgxt+Ught−1+bg),    ot=σ(Woxt+Uoht−1+bo)g_t = \tanh(W_g x_t + U_g h_{t-1} + b_g), \;\; o_t = \sigma(W_o x_t + U_o h_{t-1} + b_o)gt​=tanh(Wg​xt​+Ug​ht−1​+bg​),ot​=σ(Wo​xt​+Uo​ht−1​+bo​)
ct=ft ct−1+it gt,    ht=ottanh⁡(ct)c_t = f_t\, c_{t-1} + i_t\, g_t, \;\; h_t = o_t \tanh(c_t)ct​=ft​ct−1​+it​gt​,ht​=ot​tanh(ct​)

The derivative of the new cell state with respect to the old one along the direct path is the forget gate itself, ∂ct/∂ct−1=ft\partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = f_t∂ct​/∂ct−1​=ft​, so a gate close to 1 carries information and gradients across many steps. Forecasts for decisions

A forecast serves a decision: scheduling generation, sizing a reservoir release, ordering spare parts, or setting emission targets. Point forecasts should therefore come with uncertainty, as prediction intervals or quantiles, and probabilistic forecasting competitions in energy have made this standard practice [5]. The forecast horizon, the update frequency and the cost of errors in each direction belong in the problem statement, as the costs of misses and false alarms did in Week 8.

Formulas: Prediction intervals, quantiles and costs

If the forecast errors are roughly normal with the standard deviation σh\sigma_hσh​ at the horizon hhh, the interval

y^T+h∣T±z σh\hat{y}_{T+h \vert T} \pm z\, \sigma_hy^​T+h∣T​±zσh​

contains the future value with the chosen probability, where z=1.96z = 1.96z=1.96 for 95% and z=1.28z = 1.28z=1.28 for 80%. The spread grows with the horizon; for the naive forecast of a series whose changes are independent with the standard deviation σ\sigmaσ, it is σh=σh\sigma_h = \sigma \sqrt{h}σh​=σh​ [2]. The share of test values that fall inside the intervals, the coverage, should be close to the nominal level.

A quantile forecast qτq_\tauqτ​ states a value that the observation stays below with the probability τ\tauτ. It is scored with the pinball loss, which energy forecasting competitions use [5]:

Lτ(y,q)=τ (y−q) if y≥q,    Lτ(y,q)=(1−τ)(q−y) if y<qL_\tau(y, q) = \tau\, (y - q) \text{ if } y \ge q, \;\; L_\tau(y, q) = (1 - \tau)(q - y) \text{ if } y < qLτ​(y,q)=τ(y−q) if y≥q,Lτ​(y,q)=(1−τ)(q−y) if y<q

When an underestimate costs cuc_ucu​ per unit and an overestimate coc_oco​ per unit, the expected cost of a decision qqq is cu E[max⁡(0,Y−q)]+co E[max⁡(0,q−Y)]c_u\, \text{E}[\max(0, Y - q)] + c_o\, \text{E}[\max(0, q - Y)]cu​E[max(0,Y−q)]+co​E[max(0,q−Y)]. Raising qqq by a small amount saves cuc_ucu​ with the probability P(Y>q)P(Y > q)P(Y>q) and costs coc_oco​ with the probability P(Y<q)P(Y < q)P(Y<q), so the best decision balances both:

co P(Y≤q⋆)=cu (1−P(Y≤q⋆)),    P(Y≤q⋆)=cucu+coc_o\, P(Y \le q^{\star}) = c_u\, (1 - P(Y \le q^{\star})), \;\; P(Y \le q^{\star}) = \frac{c_u}{c_u + c_o}co​P(Y≤q⋆)=cu​(1−P(Y≤q⋆)),P(Y≤q⋆)=cu​+co​cu​​

The best decision is the quantile of the level cu/(cu+co)c_u/(c_u + c_o)cu​/(cu​+co​) of the forecast distribution, not its mean.

48±1.96×2=48±3.92,    from 44.08 to 51.92 MW48 \pm 1.96 \times 2 = 48 \pm 3.92, \;\; \text{from } 44.08 \text{ to } 51.92 \text{ MW}48±1.96×2=48±3.92,from 44.08 to 51.92 MW
