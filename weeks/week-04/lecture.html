Vagueness and fuzzy sets

Week 3 ended with a weakness of crisp rules: A threshold of 70 degrees makes a reading of 69.9 normal and a reading of 70.1 overheated. Engineers rarely think in such steps. A temperature is more or less high, a load is fairly heavy, a surface is quite rough. Zadeh proposed fuzzy sets to represent such graded concepts [1]. A fuzzy set A on a universe of discourse X is defined by a membership function that assigns every element x a degree between 0 and 1. A room at 23 degrees may belong to the set of comfortable rooms with degree 0.6 and to the set of warm rooms with degree 0.125.

Vagueness is not probability. A membership degree of 0.6 does not say that the room is comfortable with probability 0.6. It says that 23 degrees fits the concept "comfortable" to degree 0.6, and there is nothing random about it. Probability describes uncertainty about whether an event happens; fuzzy membership describes how well a precisely known value fits an imprecise concept. The two can be combined, but they answer different questions.

Common membership functions are triangular, defined by a left foot, a peak and a right foot; trapezoidal, with a flat top; and Gaussian, with a centre and a width. The support of a fuzzy set is where its membership is positive, the core is where it equals one, and an alpha-cut is the crisp set of elements whose membership is at least alpha. A linguistic variable, such as tank level, takes linguistic values, such as low, okay and high, each represented by a fuzzy set on the same universe [2].

Formulas: Membership functions

A fuzzy set AAA on a universe of discourse XXX, for example the room temperatures from 0 to 40 degrees, is defined by a membership function

μA:X→[0,1]\mu_A : X \rightarrow [0, 1]μA​:X→[0,1]

that gives every element xxx a degree of membership μA(x)\mu_A(x)μA​(x) between 0 and 1 [1]. The Greek letter mu, μ\muμ, is the usual symbol. A crisp set is the special case in which μA(x)\mu_A(x)μA​(x) is either 0 or 1. Three shapes are common. A triangular set with left foot aaa, peak bbb and right foot ccc rises linearly from aaa to bbb and falls linearly from bbb to ccc:

μ(x;a,b,c)=max⁡(min⁡(x−ab−a,c−xc−b),0)\mu(x; a, b, c) = \max\left(\min\left(\frac{x - a}{b - a}, \frac{c - x}{c - b}\right), 0\right)μ(x;a,b,c)=max(min(b−ax−a​,c−bc−x​),0)

The first fraction is the rising side, the second the falling side, the minimum picks the side on which xxx lies, and the maximum with 0 sets the membership to zero outside the feet. When a=ba = ba=b, the rising side is replaced by 1 and the set becomes a left shoulder, which keeps full membership at the low end of the universe; b=cb = cb=c gives a right shoulder. A trapezoidal set has a flat top from bbb to ccc:

μ(x;a,b,c,d)=max⁡(min⁡(x−ab−a,1,d−xd−c),0)\mu(x; a, b, c, d) = \max\left(\min\left(\frac{x - a}{b - a}, 1, \frac{d - x}{d - c}\right), 0\right)μ(x;a,b,c,d)=max(min(b−ax−a​,1,d−cd−x​),0)

A Gaussian set with centre mmm and width σ\sigmaσ (sigma) is smooth everywhere:

μ(x;m,σ)=e−(x−m)2/(2σ2)\mu(x; m, \sigma) = e^{-(x - m)^2 / (2 \sigma^2)}μ(x;m,σ)=e−(x−m)2/(2σ2)

Three crisp sets describe a fuzzy set. The support holds the elements with positive membership, the core those with full membership, and the alpha-cut AαA_{\alpha}Aα​ those with membership of at least α\alphaα:

supp(A)={x:μA(x)>0},    core(A)={x:μA(x)=1},    Aα={x:μA(x)≥α}\text{supp}(A) = \{ x : \mu_A(x) > 0 \}, \;\; \text{core}(A) = \{ x : \mu_A(x) = 1 \}, \;\; A_{\alpha} = \{ x : \mu_A(x) \ge \alpha \}supp(A)={x:μA​(x)>0},core(A)={x:μA​(x)=1},Aα​={x:μA​(x)≥α}

Operations on fuzzy sets

The classical set operations generalise. The complement NOT A has membership 1 minus the membership of A. The intersection A AND B is usually computed with the minimum of the two memberships, and the union A OR B with the maximum [1]. Other choices are possible: The product is also a valid intersection, called a t-norm, and the probabilistic sum a + b - ab is a valid union, called an s-norm. The choice changes the smoothness of the resulting controller but not the principle. Some classical laws fail on purpose: A AND NOT A need not be empty, because a temperature can be somewhat warm and somewhat not warm at the same time.

Formulas: Complement, intersection and union

For fuzzy sets AAA and BBB on the same universe, the standard operations of Zadeh work point by point [1]:

μ¬A(x)=1−μA(x)\mu_{\neg A}(x) = 1 - \mu_A(x)μ¬A​(x)=1−μA​(x)
μA∧B(x)=min⁡(μA(x),μB(x)),    μA∨B(x)=max⁡(μA(x),μB(x))\mu_{A \wedge B}(x) = \min(\mu_A(x), \mu_B(x)), \;\; \mu_{A \vee B}(x) = \max(\mu_A(x), \mu_B(x))μA∧B​(x)=min(μA​(x),μB​(x)),μA∨B​(x)=max(μA​(x),μB​(x))

For degrees of 0 and 1 they reproduce the truth tables of Week 3. The minimum is one member of a family of intersections called t-norms, functions T(a,b)T(a, b)T(a,b) of two degrees that are commutative, associative, non-decreasing in each argument and satisfy T(a,1)=aT(a, 1) = aT(a,1)=a. The product T(a,b)=abT(a, b) = a bT(a,b)=ab is another t-norm. Every t-norm has a matching union, its s-norm, obtained through the complement:

S(a,b)=1−T(1−a,1−b)S(a, b) = 1 - T(1 - a, 1 - b)S(a,b)=1−T(1−a,1−b)

For the minimum this gives the maximum. For the product it gives the probabilistic sum,

S(a,b)=1−(1−a)(1−b)=a+b−abS(a, b) = 1 - (1 - a)(1 - b) = a + b - a bS(a,b)=1−(1−a)(1−b)=a+b−ab

With these pairs the laws of De Morgan hold, for example ¬(A∧B)=¬A∨¬B\neg(A \wedge B) = \neg A \vee \neg B¬(A∧B)=¬A∨¬B, whereas the law of contradiction does not: min⁡(μ,1−μ)\min(\mu, 1 - \mu)min(μ,1−μ) is zero only when μ\muμ is 0 or 1 [1, 3].

Mamdani inference

A fuzzy rule has linguistic conditions and a linguistic conclusion: IF level error is low AND level rate is none THEN valve change is open fast. Mamdani inference evaluates a rule base in five steps [4, 5]. First, fuzzification computes the membership of each crisp input in each of its fuzzy sets. Second, each rule receives a firing strength, the minimum of the memberships in its conditions. Third, implication clips the output fuzzy set of each rule at its firing strength. Fourth, aggregation combines the clipped sets of all rules with the maximum. Fifth, defuzzification turns the aggregated fuzzy set into one crisp output, most often with the centroid, the centre of gravity of the area under the aggregated membership function.

The controller defined by a rule base is a nonlinear function from inputs to output, and its control surface can be drawn for two inputs. Overlapping membership functions make the surface smooth; more sets and rules add detail. A rule table, with one input along the rows and the other along the columns, is the usual design tool: Every cell holds the conclusion for one combination of linguistic values, and gaps in the table leave parts of the input space without any action. Symmetry of the table usually leads to symmetric behaviour of the loop.

Consider rules with two inputs, IF xxx is AkA_kAk​ AND yyy is BkB_kBk​ THEN zzz is CkC_kCk​, for k=1,…,Kk = 1, \ldots, Kk=1,…,K, and crisp inputs x0x_0x0​ and y0y_0y0​ [4, 5].

μk′(z)=min⁡(wk,μCk(z))\mu'_k(z) = \min(w_k, \mu_{C_k}(z))μk′​(z)=min(wk​,μCk​​(z))

μ(z)=max⁡kμk′(z)\mu(z) = \max_{k} \mu'_k(z)μ(z)=kmax​μk′​(z)

The integral sign ∫\int∫ adds up infinitely many thin strips; the sum on the right adds up the values at the grid points, and a finer grid brings it closer to the integral.

Takagi-Sugeno models and learning fuzzy systems

Takagi and Sugeno replaced the fuzzy conclusion by a function of the inputs: IF x is A AND y is B THEN z = p x + q y + r [6]. The output is the average of the rule outputs weighted by their firing strengths, so no defuzzification of a shape is needed. A zero-order Sugeno model has constant conclusions and behaves much like a Mamdani model with narrow output sets; a first-order model blends local linear models, which makes it attractive for modelling and for gain scheduling of controllers.

Because the Sugeno output is a differentiable function of its parameters, the parameters can be learned from data. ANFIS represents a Sugeno system as a five-layer adaptive network and trains the premise parameters with gradient descent and the linear consequent parameters with least squares [7]. Metaheuristic training is an alternative when gradients are unreliable: In one study, ANFIS supported by the vortex optimisation algorithm forecast chaotic electroencephalogram time series [8]. Such neuro-fuzzy systems link this week to the optimisation of Weeks 5 and 6 and to the neural networks of Week 10.

A first-order Takagi-Sugeno rule has a linear function as its conclusion [6]:

A zero-order rule has a constant conclusion, zk=rkz_k = r_kzk​=rk​. The firing strength wkw_kwk​ is computed as in Mamdani inference, with the minimum or with the product of the memberships, and the output is the weighted average of the rule outputs:

The normalised strengths w‾k\overline{w}_kwk​ add up to one. Its hybrid learning rule uses two facts. First, once the membership functions are fixed, zzz is linear in the consequent parameters, so these follow from a least-squares fit to the training pairs (xi,ti)(x_i, t_i)(xi​,ti​). Second, the membership parameters, such as the centre ccc of a Gaussian set, are improved by gradient descent on the squared error E=12(z−t)2E = \frac{1}{2}(z - t)^2E=21​(z−t)2 with a learning rate η\etaη (eta):

because z=w‾1z1+(1−w‾1)z2z = \overline{w}_1 z_1 + (1 - \overline{w}_1) z_2z=w1​z1​+(1−w1​)z2​ changes by z1−z2z_1 - z_2z1​−z2​ per unit of w‾1\overline{w}_1w1​, and w‾1\overline{w}_1w1​ changes by w‾1w‾2(x−c1)/σ2\overline{w}_1 \overline{w}_2 (x - c_1)/\sigma^2w1​w2​(x−c1​)/σ2 per unit of c1c_1c1​.

Fuzzy control in engineering

The first industrial success was the control of a cement kiln in Denmark, where operators' rules for burning zone temperature and oxygen were encoded as fuzzy rules [9]. The Sendai subway in Japan used predictive fuzzy control for automatic train operation, which improved riding comfort and stopping accuracy [10]. Fuzzy controllers later appeared in washing machines, cameras, heating systems, water treatment dosing and vehicle subsystems, and textbooks collect applications across civil, mechanical, chemical and electrical engineering [3].

Fuzzy control is most useful when a good mathematical model is missing but operator experience exists, when the process is nonlinear, and when smooth, explainable behaviour matters. It does not remove the need for analysis: Stability, robustness to disturbances and the effect of measurement noise must still be checked, for example by simulation over the operating range, as in the notebook. When a reliable model exists, classical or model-based control is often the better engineering choice.
