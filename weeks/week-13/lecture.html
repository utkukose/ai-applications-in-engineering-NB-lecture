Text as engineering data

A large share of engineering knowledge is written: standards and codes, design reports, maintenance and incident logs, requirements, patents and source code. Natural language processing turns such text into data. Classic tasks include search and retrieval, classification of reports, extraction of quantities and entities, summarisation and translation. Information retrieval developed many of the tools, such as term weighting and vector space models, long before neural networks [1]. Large language models add the ability to generate text and code, which changes how engineers draft, search and program, and raises new questions about accuracy and responsibility.

Formulas: Term weighting and cosine similarity

A vector space model represents a document ddd by one number per word ttt of the vocabulary. The term frequency tf(t,d)\text{tf}(t, d)tf(t,d) counts the occurrences of the word in the document. The inverse document frequency rewards words that occur in few of the NNN documents, of which df(t)\text{df}(t)df(t) contain the word [1]:

idf(t)=ln⁡Ndf(t),    w(t,d)=tf(t,d)×idf(t)\text{idf}(t) = \ln \frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}, \;\; w(t, d) = \text{tf}(t, d) \times \text{idf}(t)idf(t)=lndf(t)N​,w(t,d)=tf(t,d)×idf(t)

The library scikit-learn, used in the notebook, smooths the formula by adding 1 to both counts and to the logarithm, idf(t)=ln⁡1+N1+df(t)+1\text{idf}(t) = \ln \frac{1 + N}{1 + \text{df}(t)} + 1idf(t)=ln1+df(t)1+N​+1, so that no weight becomes zero. The notebook also uses sublinear term frequencies, 1+ln⁡tf1 + \ln \text{tf}1+lntf instead of tf\text{tf}tf, which damp words repeated within one record; for a word that occurs once, both give 1. A query is weighted in the same way, and documents are ranked by the cosine of the angle between their vectors and the query vector:

cos⁡(q,d)=q⋅d∥q∥ ∥d∥=∑tqtdt∑tqt2 ∑tdt2\cos(q, d) = \frac{q \cdot d}{\lVert q \rVert\, \lVert d \rVert} = \frac{\sum_t q_t d_t}{\sqrt{\sum_t q_t^2}\, \sqrt{\sum_t d_t^2}}cos(q,d)=∥q∥∥d∥q⋅d​=∑t​qt2​​∑t​dt2​​∑t​qt​dt​​

When all vectors are first divided by their lengths, the cosine is simply the dot product of the normalised vectors. It is 1 for the same direction and 0 when the texts share no word.

From words to vectors

A model first splits text into tokens. Whole words give huge vocabularies and fail on new words; single characters make sequences long. Byte-pair encoding starts from characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol, so that common words become single tokens while rare words split into meaningful pieces [2]. Each token is then mapped to a vector, its embedding. Word2vec showed that embeddings learned from co-occurrence capture semantic relations, so that words used in similar contexts receive similar vectors [3]. Sentence embeddings extend the idea to whole passages and support semantic search [4].

Formulas: Byte-pair merges, embeddings and the skip-gram model

Byte-pair encoding writes every word of a training corpus as a sequence of characters with an end marker, here _, and counts every pair of adjacent symbols, each occurrence weighted by the frequency of its word. The most frequent pair is merged into a new symbol everywhere, and the counting repeats until the vocabulary has the desired size [2]. A new text is split by applying the learned merges in the same order.

Each token iii of a vocabulary of size VVV owns a row eie_iei​ of an embedding matrix EEE with VVV rows and ddd columns. Multiplying the one-hot vector of the token, which has a 1 at position iii and 0 elsewhere, by EEE selects this row, so an embedding layer is a table lookup whose entries are learned.

The skip-gram model of word2vec [3] learns two vectors per word, vwv_wvw​ for the word in the centre of a window and uwu_wuw​ for the word as context. The probability that the word ooo appears near the centre word ccc is modelled as a softmax over the vocabulary:

p(o∣c)=euo⋅vc∑w=1Veuw⋅vcp(o \mid c) = \frac{e^{u_o \cdot v_c}}{\sum_{w=1}^{V} e^{u_w \cdot v_c}}p(o∣c)=∑w=1V​euw​⋅vc​euo​⋅vc​​

Training raises these probabilities for the word pairs observed in text, so that words with similar contexts end up with similar vectors.

Attention and the transformer

Attention was introduced to let a translation model look at the relevant words of the source sentence while producing each output word [5]. The transformer made attention the central operation [6]. Every token produces a query, a key and a value vector through learned linear maps. The attention weights of a token are the softmax of the dot products of its query with all keys, divided by the square root of the key dimension; its output is the weighted sum of the values. Several attention heads run in parallel and learn different relations, positional encodings inject word order, and residual connections and layer normalisation, as in the ResNets of Week 11, keep deep stacks trainable.

Two families dominate. Encoder models such as BERT see the whole text at once and are pretrained to fill in masked words, which suits classification and extraction [7]. Decoder models such as GPT are pretrained to predict the next token from the previous ones, with a causal mask that hides the future, and generate text one token at a time [8, 9]. Transformers now process images, sequences of measurements and molecules as well [10, 11].

Large language models

A language model assigns probabilities to the next token. Trained on hundreds of billions of tokens, decoder transformers develop broad abilities, and performance improves predictably with model size, data and compute, as empirical scaling laws describe [12]. GPT-3 showed that a large model can perform new tasks from a few examples in the prompt, without changing its weights [9]. Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback then align models with what users ask for [13]. Prompts that ask for intermediate reasoning steps improve performance on multi-step problems [14], and models trained on code write and explain programs [15]. Such broadly trained models, adapted to many tasks, are called foundation models [16].

Generation samples tokens from the predicted distribution. A temperature below one sharpens the distribution towards the most likely tokens; a temperature above one flattens it and increases variety and the risk of nonsense. The model has no built-in notion of truth: It produces what is probable given its training, and fluent but unsupported statements, called hallucinations, are a known failure mode [17]. For an engineer, a model's answer about a load factor or a material property is a hypothesis to check, never a source.

Formulas: Next-token probabilities, temperature, perplexity and scaling

A language model computes for the next token a score zkz_kzk​, the logit, for every token kkk of the vocabulary, and turns the scores into probabilities with a softmax [8]. A temperature TTT divides the scores first:

pk=ezk/T∑mezm/Tp_k = \frac{e^{z_k / T}}{\sum_{m} e^{z_m / T}}pk​=∑m​ezm​/Tezk​/T​

T=1T = 1T=1 gives the trained distribution, T<1T < 1T<1 sharpens it towards the most likely tokens, and T>1T > 1T>1 flattens it. Top-kkk sampling keeps only the kkk tokens with the largest scores and divides their probabilities by their sum. Training minimises the cross-entropy, the mean negative logarithm of the probability that the model gave to each actual next token xtx_txt​ of the training text, and the perplexity is its exponential:

CE=−1n∑t=1nln⁡p(xt∣x1,…,xt−1),    PP=eCE\text{CE} = -\frac{1}{n} \sum_{t=1}^{n} \ln p(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1}), \;\; \text{PP} = e^{\text{CE}}CE=−n1​t=1∑n​lnp(xt​∣x1​,…,xt−1​),PP=eCE

A perplexity of mmm means that the model is, on average, as uncertain as a choice among mmm equally likely tokens. Kaplan and colleagues found that the test loss falls as a power of the number NNN of parameters when data and compute are not limiting [12]:

L(N)=(NcN)αN,    αN≈0.076L(N) = \Big( \frac{N_c}{N} \Big)^{\alpha_N}, \;\; \alpha_N \approx 0.076L(N)=(NNc​​)αN​,αN​≈0.076

so that every doubling of the model multiplies the loss by 2−0.0762^{-0.076}2−0.076.

Retrieval-augmented generation

Retrieval-augmented generation combines a retriever with a generator [18]. The retriever searches a document collection, for example a company's standards or a project's reports, for passages relevant to the question. The generator then answers using those passages and can cite them. Grounding reduces hallucination, keeps answers current without retraining, and lets a reader verify each claim against its source. Retrieval can use sparse term weighting such as TF-IDF [1] or dense sentence embeddings [4]. Its quality can be measured like any classifier: For a set of questions with known relevant passages, the share of questions whose relevant passage appears among the top results is the hit rate.

Check your understanding. What is the main engineering benefit of retrieval-augmented generation over asking a model directly?

Formulas: Retrieval scores and their evaluation

A dense retriever encodes the question and every passage into vectors and ranks the passages by the cosines between these vectors [4]; for vectors of length 1, the cosine is simply the dot product. The generator then conditions its answer on the kkk best passages [18]. Retrieval is evaluated on test questions whose relevant passages are known [1]. If the relevant passages of QQQ questions appear at the ranks r1,…,rQr_1, \ldots, r_Qr1​,…,rQ​, the hit rate at kkk is the share of questions whose relevant passage is among the first kkk, and the mean reciprocal rank rewards passages found at a high place:

hit@k=1Q∑i=1Q[ri≤k],    MRR=1Q∑i=1Q1ri\text{hit@}k = \frac{1}{Q} \sum_{i=1}^{Q} [r_i \le k], \;\; \text{MRR} = \frac{1}{Q} \sum_{i=1}^{Q} \frac{1}{r_i}hit@k=Q1​i=1∑Q​[ri​≤k],MRR=Q1​i=1∑Q​ri​1​

where [ri≤k][r_i \le k][ri​≤k] is 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. The prompt must also fit the context window of the generator: The tokens of the instructions, the question and the kkk passages add up and must stay below the limit.

Generative models beyond text

Variational autoencoders learn a latent space from which new samples can be decoded [19]. Generative adversarial networks train a generator against a discriminator that tries to tell real from generated data [20]. Diffusion models learn to reverse a gradual noising process and now produce high-quality images; latent diffusion runs the process in a compressed space to make it efficient [21, 22]. In engineering, generative models propose designs, create synthetic training images for rare defects, and suggest candidate materials; deep learning has proposed hundreds of thousands of stable crystal structures [23], and language models have planned and run chemistry experiments with robotic equipment [24]. Every generated design still has to satisfy the physics, the codes and the tests.

Formulas: Variational autoencoders, adversarial networks and diffusion

A variational autoencoder encodes an input xxx into the mean μ\muμ and the standard deviation σ\sigmaσ of a latent variable zzz, draws z=μ+σεz = \mu + \sigma \varepsilonz=μ+σε with ε\varepsilonε from the standard normal distribution, which lets gradients pass through the sampling, and decodes zzz [19]. Responsible use in engineering work

Engineers remain accountable for their work, whatever tools they use. Four rules follow. Verify every factual or numerical claim against a primary source, a calculation or a test. Do not paste confidential designs, personal data or unpublished results into external services unless the organisation's policy allows it. Cite the sources that support the work, not the tool. And treat model inputs as an attack surface: Instructions hidden in documents can manipulate a model that reads them, and small perturbations can change the decisions of learned models [25]. The energy cost of training and running large models is also part of the engineering trade-off [26]. The course policy on the use of these tools is stated in the syllabus.

Formulas: Energy and emissions of computation

The electrical energy of a computation is the power of the hardware times the running time. Data centres need additional energy for cooling and power supply, which the power usage effectiveness PUE accounts for, and the carbon intensity ccc of the electricity converts energy into emissions [26]:

E=PUE×∑devicesP×t,    mCO2=c EE = \text{PUE} \times \sum_{\text{devices}} P \times t, \;\; m_{\text{CO}_2} = c\, EE=PUE×devices∑​P×t,mCO2​​=cE

Strubell and colleagues used a PUE of 1.58 and the average carbon intensity of the U.S. grid, 0.954 pounds, about 0.433 kg, of carbon dioxide per kWh. Both values vary strongly between data centres and countries, so an estimate should state its assumptions.
