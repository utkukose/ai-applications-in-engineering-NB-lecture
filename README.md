## About the course

This repository holds the open materials of MUH-920 Artificial Intelligence Applications in Engineering, a faculty-wide common elective of the Faculty of Engineering and Natural Sciences at Süleyman Demirel University, designed in line with the MÜDEK accreditation system. Over fourteen weeks, the course leads from intelligent agents, search and expert systems [1] through fuzzy logic [2], evolutionary and swarm optimisation [3] and machine learning [4] to deep learning [5], transformers and language models [6], reinforcement learning [7] and physics-informed learning [8]. Each method is applied to problems of the nineteen departments of the faculty, and students without programming experience learn Python along the way.

The [syllabus](SYLLABUS.md) sets out the course information and the MÜDEK alignment, the course description, the learning outcomes, the weekly plan, the assessment and the course policies. The sections below explain how the materials of a week fit together and where to find them.

## Use in other courses

These materials were prepared for the undergraduate course Artificial Intelligence Applications in Engineering (Mühendislikte Yapay Zeka Uygulamaları) at Süleyman Demirel University. They are openly available: Anyone may use and adapt them in courses of related scope and for self-study, with attribution, under the CC BY 4.0 licence for content and the MIT licence for code. Instructors can reorder weeks: Weeks 2 to 6 are largely independent of each other, Weeks 7 and 8 are the foundation for Weeks 9 to 12, and Weeks 13 and 14 build on Week 10.

## Who the course is for

The course is designed for second- and third-year undergraduate students of engineering and the natural sciences, and for anyone who wants to learn applied artificial intelligence on their own. Basic mathematics from the first year, such as functions, derivatives, vectors and elementary statistics, is enough. No programming experience is required. Students who already program can move quickly through the Python moves and spend more time on the discipline challenges and the research assignments.

## How the course works

Every week has its own folder, and its materials have distinct roles and link to each other. The week overview, the README of the folder, is the starting point: It gives the overview and the learning outcomes of the week, a concept map, the study path and the contents of the other materials, and it holds the discipline challenges, the weekly task and the optional research assignment. The lecture page explains the concepts with knowledge checks, animations, figures and review cards, and the lecture notes offer the same lecture as a printable PDF. The interactive lab turns the central idea of the week into a simulation in the browser and holds the self-assessment, the reflection prompts and the export of a learning log. The Colab notebook opens with the Python step of the week and then rebuilds the idea in Python with real or realistic data, with exercises that give immediate feedback and reference solutions in collapsed cells. Earlier work on software support for self-learning in engineering courses informed this structure [9].

Students who have never programmed start with [`start-here/`](start-here/README.md) and then follow the weeks in order. The course site at <https://utkukose.github.io/ai-applications-in-engineering-NB-lecture/> opens every week and records the progress in its knowledge checks and self-assessments in the browser.

## Python for non-programmers

Python is taught step by step alongside the engineering content, always through the tool that the week's problem needs. Each Colab notebook opens with the Python step of its week, and its later sections mark further constructs as Python moves. The fourteen steps lead from values, variables and decisions in Week 1 through lists, dictionaries, functions, NumPy arrays, classes, pandas tables, signals, tensors, images, dates and text to organising and testing code in Week 14. [PYTHON_PATH.md](PYTHON_PATH.md) describes every step and links to it, and the optional warm-up notebook in [`start-here/`](start-here/README.md) covers the absolute basics in about two hours.

## Every department, and beyond

The course addresses the departments of Computer, Environmental, Electrical and Electronics, Industrial, Food, Civil, Geophysical, Geological, Chemical, Mining, Mechanical, Automotive, Textile and Earth Sciences Engineering, and of Biology, Physics, Statistics, Chemistry and Mathematics. `DISCIPLINES.md` maps every department to the weeks with a challenge for it, links to these challenges, and adds fields not taught at the faculty, such as aerospace, biomedical, petroleum and materials engineering. `DATASETS.md` lists every public dataset used, with its source, licence notes and verification status.

## Running the materials

The notebooks run in Google Colab through the badge of each week, with no installation. For local work on Windows or Ubuntu, create a virtual environment with `python -m venv .venv`, activate it, run `pip install -r requirements.txt` and start `jupyter lab`. Datasets are downloaded on demand from their public sources; when a source is unreachable, each notebook switches to a documented synthetic stand-in so that every cell still runs. The interactive labs and lecture pages are single HTML files that work in any modern browser and store answers only in that browser. To publish the course site, enable GitHub Pages under Settings, Pages, with the main branch and the root folder. Files whose names start with a dot, such as `.nojekyll` and the workflow in `.github`, must be uploaded as well; GitHub Desktop or the command line handles them reliably.

## Reference integrity

All references were checked before release. Entries marked `web` in `references/REFERENCES.md` were confirmed against online sources such as the publisher, an indexing service, the dataset repository or arXiv during preparation; the remaining entries are standard works of the field. As an independent check, `tools/verify_references.py` compares every entry with Crossref, arXiv and OpenAlex, and the GitHub Actions workflow in `.github/workflows/verify-references.yml` runs it monthly and on every change of the bibliography, publishing a report without modifying the repository.
